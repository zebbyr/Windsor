01

The quarter in brief

Four things happened in Q2 that matter more than the headline numbers.

  • Occupancy inflected. Physical occupancy finished at 94.9% (334 of 352 units), up from 91.4% at March 31 and 220 bps ahead of budget. Forty-eight new leases commenced during the quarter, with thirteen additional approved applications in hand at quarter-end.
  • We grew rents while the market shrank them. Blended lease trade-outs of +2.1% gross / +2.8% effective across 147 leases — including positive new-lease trade-outs, at a moment when roughly half of Denver apartments are offering concessions.
  • Expense discipline carried the quarter. Controllable operating expenses finished $137K (11.9%) under budget, with contract services, repairs and maintenance, turnover, and administrative costs each landing 25–31% below plan.
  • The heavy capital is behind us. The $1.66M exterior capital program is complete. Remaining spend is concentrated in high-return interior renovations, funded from retained operating cash flow.
Why this matters

Denver is in the early innings of a recovery from a historic supply wave. Market vacancy peaked at 12.2% in late 2025 and has since compressed to 10.4%; deliveries are falling sharply; and CoStar projects a return to positive rent growth. The Windsor is entering that recovery already stabilized at 94.9% occupancy — rather than fighting to lease up into it.

Two structural initiatives sit behind these figures. A property-level insurance captive reset the insurance line from a 2025 run-rate of roughly $37,000 per month to a 2026 budget of approximately $24,000 per month. Renewals outnumbered new leases 104 to 43 — 71% of all leasing activity was retention. That is deliberate, and it aligns with what CoStar reports operators across the metro are doing:

The combination matters: the property gained 220 basis points of occupancy against budget without discounting rate to get it. Average vacant days ran 37.5 in the first half.

04

Positioning against the market

Why the property holds rate while the submarket discounts

  • Rent per square foot exceeds the submarket's luxury tier. The Windsor's in-place rent of $2.40/SF sits above the submarket's 4 & 5 Star asking rent of $2.28/SF, and 14% above its effective rent of $2.10/SF — achieved without luxury-tier lease-up risk or concession burn.
  • Unit size is a structural advantage. The Windsor averages 1,115 SF per unit against roughly 840 SF implied for the submarket and 831 SF across the last twelve months of submarket sale comparables — more than 30% larger than the competing product. The new supply pressuring this submarket is small-format 4 & 5 Star infill along the W Line and West Colfax; it does not compete for the same renter.
  • The rent roll is marked close to market. Loss-to-lease on occupied units is only 2.6% ($894,596 actual against $918,458 market rent), so reported income does not depend on an embedded mark-up to hold up.
05

Capital program and value creation

Exterior — complete

The $1.66M exterior program is finished. Roofs, structural frame repairs, slurry and concrete, three pool resurfaces, radon mitigation, keyless entry and vehicle gates, exterior lighting, mailboxes, dog park, sports court and BBQ areas have all been delivered. Deferred-maintenance and life-safety exposure has been retired, and the asset now competes on amenity quality rather than deferred condition.

Interior — accelerating

The interior renovation program covers 58 units: 49 complete, 7 in progress, 2 pending start. Across the 48 completed units with post-renovation rent data, premiums average $465 per month at a 20.6% return on cost, a payback of roughly 4.9 years. Eight units were completed during Q2 with nine more identified or underway.

Funded from cash flow

The renovation program is being funded from retained operating cash flow rather than a capital call or reserve draw. Cash on hand at June 30 was $916K (including $714K of operating cash), with approximately $845K additionally held in tax, insurance, repair and deferred-maintenance escrows.

Renovation premiums have moderated as the market has softened — the ten units completed during 2026 average approximately $334 per month at a 16.6% return on cost, against $465 and 20.6% for the completed program overall. That remains an attractive return on incremental capital, and it is the reason the program is being paced to market conditions rather than pushed through in a single campaign.

06

Submarket: Lakewood / West Corridor

The Windsor's submarket has outperformed the Denver metro throughout the downturn, and its supply pipeline has a visible, dated end point.

  • Occupancy of 91.4% (8.6% vacancy) against 89.6% for the metro — 180 basis points tighter than the market.
  • Rents held up materially better. Asking rents eased just −0.5% year over year to roughly $1,753 per month, against −1.5% for the Denver metro.
  • Supply is nearly finished. Approximately 720 units remain under construction across four projects, down from a peak of roughly 2,000 units in early 2022. Nearly all of it delivers by October 2026, and no new construction starts are scheduled until 2027.
  • This is not a supply hot zone. Only 2.8% of submarket inventory is under construction, well below Englewood/Littleton (14.6%), Broomfield County (8.6%), DTC/Southeast Corridor (7.0%), and South Douglas County (6.9%).
  • CoStar forecasts a clean recovery: vacancy tightening to 8.2% by 2027 and 7.5% by 2030, with asking rent growth returning to +3.1% in 2027 and averaging +2.5% annually through 2030.
  • Long-run stability. The submarket's historical average rent growth is 1.9% against an average vacancy of 6.5% — a low-volatility submarket, not a boom-and-bust one.

Demand drivers are durable: direct RTD W Line connectivity to downtown Denver, ongoing redevelopment along the West Colfax corridor, and the established Belmar retail and lifestyle node.

A note on the near term. CoStar's full-year 2026 forecast puts submarket vacancy at 10.0%, above today's 8.6%, as the final tranche of units delivers through October. That pressure is concentrated in the 4 & 5 Star segment, forecast at 11.2% vacancy for 2026 — a different product and a different renter than The Windsor's large-format units. The forecast reverses sharply thereafter.

07

Denver metro: the supply wave is breaking

  • Vacancy has inflected. Metro vacancy peaked at 12.2% in late 2025 and has compressed to 10.4% — occupancy up from 87.8% to 89.6%.
  • Demand is genuinely strong. 12,044 units absorbed over the trailing twelve months, roughly 20% above the pre-pandemic five-year average, with Q2 2026 the strongest absorption reading on record.
  • 2026 is the first year in five in which demand is projected to outpace new supply.
  • Rent growth is turning. −1.5% year over year, improved from −3.3% in 2025. CoStar forecasts +1.9% in 2026 and +3.2% in 2027, with occupancy improving toward 91% by 2030.
  • Future supply faces structural barriers. Denver's Affordable Housing Policy (an 8–15% affordable set-aside on developments of ten or more units), permitting delays, and elevated construction and labor costs have caused new starts to fall sharply. The next supply wave is years away.

Outlook

No disposition is planned; the property is in year three of a five-year hold. Ownership continues to evaluate potential recapitalization opportunities that may result in a liquidity event, and will provide a detailed update should we move closer to a signed term sheet. In the meantime the operating plan is unchanged: continue the interior renovation program, hold expense discipline, and convert the occupancy gain into sustained rent growth as the submarket's supply pipeline clears through October.
